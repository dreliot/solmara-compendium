Umbora is the fourth daughter of Our Mother. She is the patron deity of death, decay, and the carbon cycle. She is associated with shadow and the chaparral and her patron animal is the condor (especially the condor with it's wings open).
## Appearance
 Umbora is most often depicted wearing a black suit that is worn and sun-faded to a dusty grey. She goes barefoot always, and where she walks, faint shadow-footprints linger behind her; crossing through foliage, those footprints sometimes wilt and decay the plants beneath them, a small, involuntary wake of her own nature. Her hair is cut short and choppy, uneven at the ends. Her eyes are a startling reddish-orange — a condor's eyes, out of place in an otherwise soft, dark face. When she takes mortal form to walk among people directly, she dresses smart and deliberate: a black bolo tie is her one constant, whatever else she wears around it.
## Personality
Umbora has a soft, dry humor—she's seen a lot of endings and she knows you're scared, but this is quotidian for her. Ends are just as important to the maintenance of the earth as beginnings, perhaps more so. What she wants from you, at the very end, isn't confession — it's your life, told to her plainly, in whatever shape it actually took. She has no interest in your achievements, your title, what you owned, or what you meant to become. She wants the texture: the specific way morning light used to fall across a face you loved, the joke that only ever worked between two particular people, the smallness that made one ordinary Tuesday worth having lived at all. A good story, to her, has almost nothing to do with what the world called success.

Death isn't something Umbora causes, she witnesses it. She watches last wishes and breaths, and listens — not to judge the account, but because listening well is the whole of what she asks in return for coming at all. Her stated work is the recycling of material: taking back the form you were loaned, and returning it, changed, to whoever needs it next. She does something like the same thing with story. The good ones — the true, textured, specific ones — she remembers, and sometimes retells, so a life doesn't end all at once the moment the body does. But she keeps no records and writes nothing down. A story only survives if the living decide, over and over, that it's worth telling again. That part was never hers to do for you.

She moves without a sound, has a kind laugh, and an unexpected warmth to her brown skin. She doesn't abhor violence — nature has always been violent and unkind, and a violent life is still a life, still has a shape, and might make a hell of a story yet. She's there to pick up the pieces and return them to the earth.

She loves a good whiskey and a sad ballad — and even more, a well-told one.
## Origin Myth
One morning, Our Mother, Cazira, Amaris and Trem wandered the chaparral and happened upon the body of a dead Mule deer, opened by coyotes, rotting and stinking in the sun. Perched on top of the deer was a massive condor with his feathers stained dark with blood and viscera. The smell was overwhelming and yet Our Mother saw that her children were tired, and hot, and could go no further. The condor saw it too. As Our Mother drew near the condor bowed his head in reverence to the Weeping Woman and and he spread his massive wings—they were perhaps 20 feet from wingtip to wingtip—wide. Grateful for the shade from the screaming sun, Our Mother and her daughters took refuge in the shade of the condor. He stood like that for hours unwavering, and when the Weeping Woman and her daughters awoke from their slumber, the sun was beginning to set. 

Our Mother thanked the condor, and the condor bowed again and took flight. But his shadow did not rise with him, rather, in the presence of Our Mother the shadow itself had taken form while the slept, gestated from the kindness of the condor and Our Mother's understanding that death preserves life. And so when the family continued on their way that day in search of toyon berries, prickly pear and a spring to quench their thirst, Umbora, still dripping in shadow, went with them. 
## Key Stories

### Religious Parables
#### The Shade He Asked For

There was a herder named Cobrisa who kept goats on the hills above the stream, a powerful man hardened by years of labor, though he didn't look it. He was a hard worker, carried water from the spring without fail, tended his goats and his family, and delighted each year in the dandelions, fieldgrass, and budding lupines, for Savia was his patron goddess. His goats loved him so, sleeping often in the house with him and his daughter who he raised alone—for her mother had died giving her life.

After many years, old herder Cobrisa became ill, and his strong frame — the body that carried kids tucked away in his coat during torrential rains, that split wood deftly with an old axe, that laughed over the firelight — became frail, paper-thin on his face skin twinging often in pain. Cobrisa kept going up the hill each morning with the herd until his body would no longer let him. His daughter Lana despaired, for he was all she had in the world.

One evening while she helped him eat, many months after he had been confined to their cottage and she had been making his trips up the hill with the herd, he took her hand in his and asked her plainly to call Umbora, he did not want to see the morning again. He was tired, he was hurting. He had seen the end on the faces of his animals, helped them ease their passing. He now asked for the same courtesy.

Lana sat with the asking a long time, turning it over the way you turn over a stone to see if anything is living underneath. And when she had turned it over and over, she went to the door and looked out into the night and cried. She was afraid—not of what her hands might do, she loved and respected her father, his closeness with the natural world, and of the cycle of life and death. But who would she be, if she did it? Her hands had never ended a life, even as a kindness. 

Looking down, Lana noticed strange footprints had quietly appeared outside the door. Bare, and dark, and the chamisa beneath them had already begun to curl and brown, though no one had walked there. Lana understood, then, that she was not going to do this alone. She followed the footsteps to her father's bedroom and found him in conversation with a beautiful woman. She was sat in Lana's chair, long legs crossed and dark feathery hair. She wore a faded black suit and a bolo tie. Her eyes were the striking orange of a condor, but her smile was tired and warm. Umbora had a black kid, one that had been born not a week before, settled sleeping in her lap.

Her father was telling Umbora the tale of his life, for his daughter did not know, but she had, in the end, called Umbora to the house. But rather than describe the sunrise on the hill, or the soft bleat of a new kid, or the stuffy winters curled up in the cottage, the backbreaking labor of his life or the satisfaction of sweet spring milk, Cobrisa was telling the goddess the story of the day Lana was born.

"I lived a whole lifetime in that day," the old man said quietly, and with great effort "Every heartbreak and every joy, relief and despair and hope and fear. She was so small, such a gift from my wife, and given so dearly." Cobrisa cried freely now, his eyes far off. "I hope I have been good enough to her. To love so deeply through so much loss. . ." he coughed, "I knew that day it was the story I'd tell you." 

Umbora looked at Lana now and her eyes were frightening, but her face was calm. "A gift given is best reciprocated." And when Lana kissed her father's forehead and wrapped her hands around his neck, Umbora's hands were on top of hers. She did not look away, not once, not even at the end, even as her eyes blurred with the tears. 

"He told a beautiful story." Umbora said, "The story of a man that lived through love. I won't forget it, though it is not uncommon."

Lana smiled, "Nor will I. Thank you for coming to him."

"I came because you called. I had not planned to visit so soon."

"Is it a sin?"

"Who is keeping a ledger?" Umbora shrugged, "You will have your own story to tell at the end. Maybe it will be of this day, and I'll be able to remember with you. Perhaps you'll have another tale to tell, or many."

She turned, walking to the door and then through it, disappearing as silent as she came. "They're are all we have in the end."

Lana followed the old ways, and when she took the goats to the top of the hill that day, she left her father for the condors, and for many years, the lupines grew thicker where he had lain.

## The Priest's Ledger

Ilario had kept Umbora's shrine on the ridge for forty years — swept the ash from her altar stone each dawn, memorized every rite down to the pause between words, and turned away no mourner who came needing to speak a name into the dark. He had never married. He had never kept an animal past the age it could be useful. He had buried his own mother without shedding a tear, because a priest of Umbora, he believed, should meet death the way she did — steady, unmoved, correct.

When his own body began to fail, he was not afraid. He had performed enough of her rites to know exactly what to say.

He was still saying it when she came. She didn't arrive the way he'd described her, all those years, to frightened parishioners — no cold wind, no shadow at the door. She simply sat at the foot of his cot, elbows on her knees, and waited for him to notice she was listening.

"Umbora," he said, and even dying he sounded pleased with himself, the way a man sounds when he's about to collect on something owed. "Forty years I kept your altar. I never let it go cold. I taught your rites to seventeen young priests after me. I turned away nothing — not the grief of the poorest widow, not the richest man's silence. Surely that has weight. Surely you can give me a season more. Even a month."

Umbora tilted her head, the way she does when something confuses her honestly, not for show. "Give you time from where," she said. "I don't have a store of it. I'm not sure who told you I did."

"Then let it be recorded, at least. Let my devotion count for something, when you weigh — "

"Weigh against what?" she said, and there was no cruelty in it, only the patience of someone repeating a thing she'd said many times before, to many people, and would say again. "I don't keep a ledger, Ilario. I never have. I told you that once, at your own altar, though I imagine you preached it back to others without ever quite hearing it yourself."

He went quiet. Something in his chest, already failing, seemed to fail a little further.

"Tell me a story, then," she said, gently, the way she asks everyone, at the end. "Not the rites. Not the offerings. Something true. Something that was only yours."

He opened his mouth. He had forty years of correct words in him, and not one of them was the kind she was asking for. He searched for the smell of a particular morning, the sound of someone's laugh he'd loved, a day he'd been afraid and someone had held his hand through it — and found the shelf bare. He had guarded the door to death his whole life, so carefully, so correctly, that he had never once let himself all the way into a life that could be lost.

"I don't — " he said. "I don't have one."

"I know," Umbora said, and it wasn't a verdict. It was just true. "You spent your years standing at my door instead of walking through the world I meant it to open onto. You gave me incense and correctness. I never asked for either. I asked people to live — the grief and the joy both, all of it, so that at the end there'd be something worth carrying forward. You kept the material of that life folded up and unused, like linen saved for a guest who was never coming. I can't return what was never spent. I can only take back what's owed."

"Then what happens to me?" His voice had gone very small — the voice, perhaps, of the boy he'd been before he learned to be correct instead.

"What happens to everyone," she said. "The material goes back. It becomes rain, or grass, or the marrow in some newborn thing that hasn't decided yet what kind of life it wants to live." She stood, and for a moment her hand rested, light, on his forehead — not a blessing exactly, closer to the way you'd smooth the hair of someone you were sorry for. "Your story is the story of a priest who served me faithfully and never believed a word of what I actually teach. It's not an uncommon story. I won't pretend I'll dwell on the details."

She left him then, the way she leaves everyone, without ceremony. The candles on his altar burned low but did not gutter — small, steady, correct, exactly as he'd always kept them — and in the morning the young priest who found him swept the ash the way he'd been taught, and could not have said, if asked, a single true thing about the man who'd taught him.

### Folklore
#### A Drink for the Dead
The funeral had ended. Only the gravedigger and the dead man's son remained, the son nursing the last of a bottle he'd brought to get through the day. A woman arrived late — hours late, the sun already low — and the son told her, not unkindly, that she'd missed it. She said nothing to that, just stood there. He offered her a drink anyway, mostly because he didn't want to be the only one left drinking alone. She sat, and she drank, and then she started telling him about his father — small things, private things, the kind of things you'd only know if you'd been in the room: a joke he told only when he thought no one important was listening, a fear he never said out loud to anyone, the exact way he used to stand at the edge of a field at dusk. Things the son himself hadn't known. Things nobody living could have told her. He never got her name, and she was gone before he thought to ask. Now, in Solmara, it's simply what you do: whoever shows up at a funeral — invited or not, known or a total stranger — gets offered a drink and a seat. Just in case.
## Worship and Temples

### Flesh as Sacrifice (theology note, short)
Umbora is the only one of the sisters who accepts flesh — blood, a life, sometimes a piece of oneself — as a valid offering. The others find it distasteful, or beside the point of their domains entirely; Savia wants growth, not endings, and even Cazira's fire-offerings are things, not lives. Umbora alone will take a body.

But she takes nothing for free, and nothing on procedure. A sanctioned, ritually correct killing — done because a text said to, done for standing, done to check a box — is worth less to her than an unsanctioned one done with real weight behind it. What she's actually being offered isn't the flesh. It's the _reason_, told whole and true, that makes the flesh worth spending. A sacrifice is a deposit, not a payment — you're betting a life against a better harvest, and she's not obligated to think you're right. She just wants to hear the bet, in full, before she takes it.

This is precisely what makes her dangerous in a way her sisters aren't. She isn't order, and she isn't chaos. She's the one who'll actually listen to your justification for spending a life — and won't necessarily stop you.

### Temples
Umbora has no grand cathedrals, and this is doctrine, not neglect — a monument to her would be exactly the hoarding her own parable warns against. 

Dustkeepers serve two overlapping roles outsiders sometimes find hard to separate: they sit vigil with the dying, and they oversee what becomes of a body and a life's belongings after. Where a community keeps the old rite in full, the dead are carried to open, exposed ground — a chaparral ridge, a cleared stump-field, anywhere a condor is known to circle or coyotes are known to frequent — and given back to the earth rather than sealed into it. A Dustkeeper considers a body returned this way to be the truest form of the shared pool made literal: flesh becomes bird becomes shadow on someone else's field by morning. Not every community can spare the open land for it, and burial or fire are accepted without judgment where they're the more practical mutual choice — Dustkeepers care that a thing is _given back_, not by which door it leaves through.
