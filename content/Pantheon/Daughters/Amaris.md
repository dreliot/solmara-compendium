The second of the sister goddesses. Patron of water's movement and force (fresh and salt water), heavily associated with mirrors and reflection. Twin to Cazira: as a young child, Cazira looked into a tide pool and saw Amaris as her own reflection looking back; when she reached in, she drew her twin to the surface. This origin binds fire and water together at the cosmological level, not just thematically.
## Appearance
Amaris in her human form is short, usually curvy with long, thick wavy dark hair and golden skin. She has striking blue eyes which seem to shift and reflect the light like a rippling brook. She dresses femininely, in flowing dresses and robes, often with ribbons in her hair. She has a high, clear laugh and laughs often.
## Personality
Amaris is light, bubbly, and generally focused on the frolic. The trickster goddess of the Pantheon, she often reshapes the world around her—she loves a lie or a clever word as much as the patient devotion of the sailor. Water is the essence of change, and Amaris delights in being changeable. She, like her twin Cazira, sees herself as truly and fundamentally above humanity, and sometimes overlooks that the players in her game are living things.

Still, Amaris' playful nature is mostly good-natured, mostly without malice, mostly in search of new experience, joy, and revelation. She delights in her trickery, and has been known to grant both boons and banes. She is delighted by a good ruse the way Cazira is delighted by a good fight. She loves riddles that have two right answers and only one good outcome, bargains with a clause nobody reads closely enough, disguises worn not to hide but to test who's paying attention. She isn't malicious by default; she's curious. 

That said, she keeps a code, even if she'd never call it one. She doesn't lie to punish honesty, and she has real respect for anyone clever enough to catch her at it — a caught trick delights her more than a successful one. Smugglers, con artists, spies, and diplomats all claim her, usually correctly. So do sailors, less because she's kind to them and more because a smart sailor knows better than to trust calm water, and Amaris would rather be respected than trusted.

She has no interest in Umbora's stillness or Savia's nurturing. She's quick, restless, and always three moves ahead — not because she's plotting against you, but because sitting still has never once occurred to her as an option.

### Origin Myth
Before Amaris was a goddess, she was a girl-shaped stillness at the bottom of a pool — not born, not made, just _there_, the way water is there before anyone names it a lake. She had no want of her own until the day Cazira, still small, still more curious than dangerous, wandered off from her mother and knelt at the water's edge to look at her own face.

What looked back was not quite a reflection. It blinked a half-beat late. It smiled before Cazira did, not after. Amaris had found a game, and the game was this: to be exactly what Cazira expected to see, and nothing more — right up until the moment it would be funnier to be something less.

For days this went on. Cazira came back to the pool again and again, delighted by her own reflection without knowing why it felt like company. She talked to it. It answered, in the smallest ways a ripple can answer — a tilt of the head a moment too soon, a wave that curled the wrong direction for the wind. Cazira, firstborn and already sharp, began to notice. One afternoon she moved her hand one way and watched her reflection's hand go another, and she understood, all at once, that she had been played.

Most children would have been frightened. Cazira laughed — a short, delighted bark of a laugh, the first sound of real recognition between the twins — and plunged her hand into the water to grab the thing that had been fooling her. She caught hold of something that was almost a wrist, and pulled.

What came up was not water shaped like a girl. It was a girl, entire — but unfinished, the way a wave is unfinished before it breaks, all momentum and no shore to spend it on. The Mother, watching from nearby, saw what her daughter had caught and understood what it meant: a thing clever enough to deceive a god's child, brave enough to let itself be caught, was already most of the way to being a person. She knelt at the water's edge and finished what the game had started, and Amaris drew her first true breath laughing, still mid-trick, delighted that the trick had worked exactly as far as it needed to and not one step further.

This is why the sisters bind fire and water at the root: not two forces meeting by accident, but one twin catching the other at her own game, and a mother who thought that was reason enough to let her live. It is also, the priesthood holds, the truest thing anyone has ever said about Amaris: she was born already knowing that being caught is not the same as losing.
