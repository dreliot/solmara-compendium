Cazira is the goddess of fire and sun and the firstborn of the Mother’s children. Nature predates the goddesses: they did not create the forces of the world, but came to represent and wield them. All forms of fire are different faces of Cazira, and different cults and regions emphasize different interpretations of her.
## Birth

Before the Mother was a goddess, she was simply a woman. She was provided for by a bountiful, untouched earth and was a friend to every bird, beast, and fish. She took no more than she needed and gave no less than was asked. Yet every creature had language to sing to another, and although she could mimic their songs, she could never understand them. She longed for someone to sing to.

Overcome with grief, the woman sat beneath a tiny redwood sapling and wept. Her tears watered the tree until it grew tall enough to touch the heavens. The tree carried her sadness to the stars, and the stars answered with a storm, weeping alongside her.

A bolt of lightning split the redwood in two, and it began to burn continuously. At the center of the burn was a heart that the fire would not consume. The woman ate the burning heart and felt a stirring in her womb. Months later, Cazira was born.

This was the moment the woman became the Mother. Her divinity arrived through grief, longing, and a strange communion with fire.
## Character

Cazira is authoritative, boastful, and courageous. She takes charge, including over her twin, Amaris. The two are the closest of the sisters and also the most bitter rivals. Amaris has a softer disposition and usually allows Cazira to have her way.

When the Mother left her daughters in charge, Cazira felt betrayed and abandoned. She believed that the Mother had summoned her children into the world to soothe her own loneliness, only to leave them with the same loneliness she could not stand. Cazira’s grief manifests as apoplectic rage: against her sisters, against the mortal world, and against nature itself.
## Regional Interpretations

In Northern Solmara, which did not burn as badly during Ashfall, Cazira is seen as softer. There she is associated with the warmth of a wood stove during a perilous winter.

In Southern Solmara, her persona is more wrathful. She is associated with burning manzanita and fleeing rabbits. Some people see her as a kind of evil goddess, and a few fringe groups attach a stigma to her worship.
## Worship

Cazira’s worship is the least common among the five sisters. Her devotees include firekeepers, cooks, farmers, and some smiths, although most smiths follow Trem.

Every established temple of Cazira keeps a flame that is never allowed to go out. Some devotees keep a similar candle in their homes. Practices differ: keeping the candle lit continuously is considered the purest form of the practice, but in fire-prone areas it is often seen as reckless. Many devotees instead light it only during prayer.

Worshippers pray over the lighting of a flame and burn offerings of sage and manzanita. People often pray to Cazira to avoid fire rather than to call upon it. After a good harvest, farmers may burn a portion of their crops to thank her for the sun.

## Cazira and [[The Ashfall Years | Ashfall ]]

It is not uncommon for religious Solmaraans to believe that Cazira caused Ashfall through her grief and rage over the Mother’s departure. This belief can coexist with environmental explanations for the disaster. Most of Cazira’s followers believe that nature fell out of balance and that mortals must do more to protect that balance.

Her most extreme followers believe that Ashfall was necessary because nature needed the burning. Some embrace this darker face of Cazira and advocate for further burns.

A fringe belief known as the **Earthbound Doctrine** holds that Cazira’s four sisters imprisoned her beneath the earth, bringing Ashfall to an end. According to the doctrine, she remains there, waiting. No one knows the location of her prison, and most believers think it must remain a mystery lest Ashfall begin again.

## Depiction

Cazira is depicted as a beautiful woman with long red hair, gold eyes, full lips, and a sharp, often laughing face. She does not resemble the Mother. She is frequently shown emerging from the burning redwood, sometimes with blackened hands. Her hands refer both to her birth from the burning heart and to fire’s destructive power.

The redwood and manzanita are especially associated with her.
